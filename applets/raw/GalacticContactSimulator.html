One-way signal arrivals

    How the model works
    • Each civilization gets a random birth time within the time window and a random position in a thin galactic disk. Its lifetime is drawn from the chosen distribution with the mean you set.
    • Signals travel at the speed of light (1 light-year per year). Message received: some moment in the sender's life exists at which a signal reaches the other civilization while it is alive, even if the sender is already gone. Conversation: the reply must also get back while the first sender is alive.
    • "Chance a possible link is noticed" stands in for the search problem: pointing at the right star, at the right frequency, at the right time. At 100% the result is an upper limit on what is physically possible.
    • Light bubbles. When a civilization starts to communicate, a bubble of signal grows around it at the speed of light: its edge is the first-contact surface. When the civilization ends, the signals it already sent keep traveling, so a second surface, the last-contact surface, leaves the place where it died. Between the two surfaces is a ring (a shell in 3D) where the civilization can still be heard after it shuts down. A hollow marker identifies the source of those signals. The picture is flat, so the radius is the distance traveled. An arrow runs from the sender to the civilization receiving its signal. A dashed arrow marks a one-way arrival. A solid arrow marks an arrival that can be answered while the sender is still awake. The statistics follow the "What counts as contact" setting. On the whole-window time scale the rings cross the galaxy within a tiny fraction of a time step, so zoom in to see them.
    • The Sun. Tick "Include the Sun" and we become one more civilization, placed where the Sun really is in the disk. It awakens at the time you set and lasts as long as you set. It is drawn as a white diamond, grows its own bubbles, and hears or is heard like any other. The contact estimates show the chance at the Sun for the selected contact definition, alongside separate one-way estimates. The Sun is not counted in the other statistics.
    • The Sun as a point. For a point in space at the Sun's position, we ask when it lies between the first-contact and last-contact surfaces of some civilization, which is when a signal from that civilization is passing over it. The panel gives the chance that this happens at some moment of a random period of the length you choose (default 100 years), and the share of all time the Sun is covered.
    • Results are averages over many simulated galaxies. The galaxy drawn above is one of them.
    • Machine scenarios vary the share of long-lived communicators and their mean lifetime. Each simulated civilization has one continuous active period.
    • Simplifications: no expansion or probes, no change of birth rate with cosmic time, no habitability zones beyond the position presets, and "reach" is a hard cutoff rather than a power budget.
